# Decomposition, Mathematical Thinking, and Life Transfer

## Central product purpose

Math is the training ground. Decomposition is the transferable life skill.

The product should teach the learner how to:

1. face an unfamiliar problem without immediately giving up or guessing;
2. identify the desired outcome;
3. separate what is known from what is unknown;
4. break the unknown into smaller questions;
5. choose the smallest useful next action;
6. solve one part at a time;
7. reconstruct the partial results into a complete solution;
8. verify that the final result answers the original problem.

> The learner does not need to solve the entire problem immediately. The learner needs to find and complete the next useful step.

This principle applies to mathematics, but also to presentations, studying, creative work, planning, technical problems, decisions, and other complex tasks.

## Two connected curricula

The product needs two parallel curriculum layers.

```text
Mathematics curriculum
What mathematical knowledge should the learner acquire?

Thinking curriculum
What way of thinking should the learner develop through that mathematics?
```

The thinking curriculum is not optional motivational content. It should be mapped across mathematical topics and assessments from grade 4 through grade 12.

## Decomposition as a measurable competency

Decomposition should be tracked independently from content mastery.

A learner may understand arithmetic operations but still be unable to structure an unfamiliar word problem.

The system should observe whether the learner can:

- identify the final goal;
- identify relevant and irrelevant information;
- separate knowns and unknowns;
- create useful subproblems;
- order subproblems;
- choose the next action;
- solve each component;
- reconnect partial results;
- verify the reconstructed result;
- perform the process independently.

## Decomposition assistance ladder

Responsibility should gradually move from the agent to the learner.

### Stage 1 — Agent decomposes

The agent shows the complete sequence of smaller steps.

### Stage 2 — Learner selects the first step

The learner chooses which smaller question should be answered first.

### Stage 3 — Learner orders prepared steps

The learner arranges a set of actions into a useful sequence.

### Stage 4 — Learner selects useful subproblems

The learner distinguishes useful smaller questions from distracting or unnecessary ones.

### Stage 5 — Learner creates the decomposition

The learner proposes the smaller questions or tasks required to solve the main problem.

### Stage 6 — Independent decomposition

The learner structures and solves an unfamiliar problem without decomposition support.

Progress should be measured partly by how much support was required.

## Core mathematical thinking principles

Each mathematical topic should be connected to one or more transferable thinking competencies.

### Decomposition

Break a large problem into manageable parts.

### Known and unknown separation

Determine what information exists, what is missing, and what is irrelevant.

### Next-action selection

Choose the smallest action that creates useful progress.

### Abstraction

Remove surface details and preserve the underlying relationship or structure.

### Representation switching

Express the same idea through words, objects, diagrams, tables, graphs, number lines, or symbols.

### Structural pattern recognition

Recognize shared mathematical relationships rather than relying on familiar layout, colors, wording, or answer position.

### Estimation and sense checking

Predict an approximate result and evaluate whether the final answer is reasonable.

### Assumptions and constraints

Identify what must be true, what is fixed, and where a method or model stops being valid.

### Reversibility

Work backward, use inverse operations, and verify a result by retracing the process.

### Evidence and proof

Distinguish a justified conclusion from a guess, authority claim, or repeated example.

### Counterexamples and falsification

Test whether a proposed rule fails in at least one valid case.

### Systematic enumeration

Consider all relevant possibilities without omission or duplication.

### Optimization and trade-offs

Find the best available choice under limited time, cost, information, or other constraints.

### Proportional thinking

Reason about relative quantities, rates, scales, percentages, and ratios rather than only absolute values.

### Thinking under uncertainty

Distinguish possible from probable and make decisions when information is incomplete.

### Local and global thinking

Understand how small changes accumulate and how local actions affect a larger system.

### Invariants

Identify what remains unchanged while representations, forms, or procedures change.

### Iteration and correction

Use failed attempts as evidence, change strategy, and try again deliberately.

### Precision

Use definitions, symbols, units, and conditions carefully enough to prevent ambiguity.

### Reconstruction

Combine solved parts into a coherent result and verify it against the original goal.

| Mathematical area | Transferable thinking principles |

| Arithmetic | decomposition, estimation, checking, reversibility |
| Fractions and ratios | proportional thinking, invariants, representation |
| Word problems | translation, knowns and unknowns, modelling, reconstruction |
| Algebra | abstraction, constraints, reversibility, decomposition |
| Geometry | spatial representation, proof, invariants, precision |
| Functions | relationships, input/output thinking, prediction, change |

| Trigonometry | indirect measurement, representation, modelling |
| Calculus | local change, accumulation, optimization |

## Three types of mastery

The system should distinguish:

```text
Content mastery
Understands the mathematical concept.

Thinking mastery
Can decompose, represent, reason, select strategies, and verify.

Transfer mastery
Can apply the same thinking principle in a different or real-life context.
```

A learner should not be considered fully independent merely because a familiar mathematical exercise was completed correctly.

## Life-transfer insights

Life-transfer messages should be short, relevant, and connected to something the learner just accomplished.

Examples:

### Presentation planning

> You turned one difficult problem into four small actions. A presentation works the same way: research, choose the message, create the slides, and practise. Today you only need the first useful step.

### Creative work

> A finished drawing looks complex, but it can be separated into pose, basic shapes, face, clothes, and details. That is the same decomposition skill.

### Evaluating claims

> You did not accept the rule because it worked twice. You looked for evidence and tested it. That is how you check whether an online claim is reliable.

### Planning backward

> You solved this by reversing the operations. Deadlines can also be handled backward: final result, required parts, then today’s first task.

These insights should appear after meaningful breakthroughs, during reflection, when motivation is low, or when the learner asks why a topic matters. They should not interrupt every exercise or become generic lectures.

## Thinking Competency Graph

The product should maintain a second graph alongside the mathematical competency graph.

```text
Mathematical Competency Graph
fractions → ratios → proportional equations

Thinking Competency Graph
representation → proportional reasoning → modelling

Life Transfer Library
budgeting → planning → evidence → decision-making
```

The layers connect:

```text
math skill
↔ thinking competency
↔ life context
```

Each mathematical skill should eventually define:

- mathematical outcome;
- thinking competencies trained;
- common anti-patterns;
- life-transfer examples;
- evidence required for content mastery;
- evidence required for thinking mastery;
- evidence required for transfer mastery.

## Scope

The initial product implementation remains focused on Bulgarian grades 4–8 and remediation of earlier foundations.

The thinking-curriculum horizon should extend through grade 12 so later algebra, functions, statistics, probability, geometry, trigonometry, modelling, and calculus can be mapped to increasingly advanced reasoning and life-transfer principles.

## Product north star

> The learner can face a large unfamiliar problem, identify what she knows, break the unknown into manageable parts, choose the next useful action, solve progressively, reconstruct the result, and verify it independently.

Mathematics provides the measurable environment where this way of thinking is trained. The long-term value is the learner beginning to approach the rest of life in the same way.
